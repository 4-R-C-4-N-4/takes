# Piece mobility vs cost (ruleset 2026.5)

Generated by `pnpm --filter @takes/catalog mobility` (`tools/catalog/scripts/mobility.ts`).
It counts the squares a piece can move to or capture on.

- **Centre** is the average over d4/e4/d5/e5 on an empty board.
- **Orth / Diag / Leap** splits that centre count by line type.
- **Whole board** averages all 64 squares.
- **Corner** is from a1.
- **Busy 25% / 50%** use random positions with that share of the other squares occupied. Sliders stop at the first piece (which they may capture); leaps ignore blockers. This is the closest of these columns to middlegame reality.
- **Screen strength** is the four-way catalog screen: score versus the piece it replaced, from 160 games, with ±0.08 noise. Minors replaced the knights, the rook-tier piece replaced the rooks, majors replaced the queen. Compare it only within a tier. Coroner was screened in the knight slot.

## Findings

1. **Coroner (BN, 7) and Scarab (B3N, 5) are almost the same piece in a real game.**
   - Coroner's only extra power is a bishop ray past three squares.
   - Centre counts are 21 vs 20, and only on an empty board does Coroner pull ahead (14.0 vs 12.1 over the whole board).
   - With 25% of squares occupied the gap is 11.4 vs 10.8; at 50% it's 9.9 vs 9.7.
   - Their screen scores in the knight slot (+0.44 vs +0.35) sit about one noise band apart.
   - So the 2-point gap pays for long diagonals, and those only matter on open boards and in endgames. That supports Ivy's read that Coroner is weak at 7, or that Scarab is cheap at 5.
2. **Octopus (R3B3) and Impact (R2B) are the busiest minors yet cost 5.**
   - On a 25%-occupied board they reach 12.6 and 12.0 squares. That's more than Coroner at 7 (11.4) and much more than the Rook at 5 (8.7).
   - Their screen scores (+0.36, +0.40) agree. They are the first candidates to go up.
3. **The Rook's strength isn't raw square count.**
   - It reaches 14 squares from anywhere, corners included, so it is never out of play.
   - Its lines run the full length of the board: to the promotion rank, along the back rank and down open files. It also never loses access to half the squares the way a Bishop does.
   - It suffers most from crowding (8.7 at 25% occupancy, 6.0 at 50%). That is the classic pattern: quiet early, decisive once the board opens.
   - Snapshot mobility undervalues it, which fits Ivy's sense that full row and column coverage is the strongest movement.
4. **Leapers keep their mobility in crowded positions.**
   - Halo, Jester, Turtle and Knight have nearly identical counts at 25% and 50% occupancy.
   - Halo and Jester at 4 reach about 1.5× the Knight's squares on a busy board.
5. **Bane at 15 is priced at 1.67× the Queen; its busy-board mobility is 1.34× hers.**
   - The knight jumps add forks and mating power that square counts miss, so mobility alone suggests 15 may now be slightly high rather than low.
6. **Squares per point isn't flat, and shouldn't be.**
   - Minors give about 2.0–2.5 busy squares per point; major pieces give 1.3–1.7.
   - Combined long-range lines (mating power, decisive endgames) are worth more per square.
   - Use per-point figures to compare pieces within a tier, not across tiers.

## Limits

Mobility is one input, not a price. It ignores:
- forks (leapers);
- being stuck on one square colour (Bishop);
- retreat and safety;
- king attack;
- whether the piece can mate alone.

Self-play valuation should settle the 2026.6 numbers. See `docs/PRICING-BACKLOG.md`.
